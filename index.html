What is a black hole?

A black hole is a region of space where gravity pulls so hard that nothing can escape it. It forms when a huge amount of matter is crushed into an incredibly small space. Not even light — the fastest thing in existence — can break free once it falls too close.

The point of no return

The event horizon is the edge of a black hole. Cross it, and there is no path back out — every direction leads inward. It is the boundary of a region the universe hides from us completely.

04

A disk of fire

Gas and dust falling toward a black hole spiral into a flat, blazing disk. Friction heats this matter to millions of degrees until it glows. The black hole's gravity then bends that light, wrapping the disk into a halo around the dark.

10 · The long goodbye

Do black holes last forever?

Empty space is never truly empty. Quantum theory says pairs of particles — one matter, one antimatter — constantly flicker into existence everywhere and, almost always, annihilate an instant later.

But right at the event horizon, one of the pair can fall in while its partner escapes. That escaping particle carries real energy away as Hawking radiation — and to balance the books, the black hole must surrender an equal sliver of its own mass. Repeat this for long enough and the black hole evaporates.

The twist: a smaller black hole is hotter and radiates faster, so as it shrinks it vanishes ever quicker — ending not in a slow fade, but a final, brilliant burst.

This is a giant supermassive black hole — so large that its tidal forces are gentle enough to cross the horizon intact. Near a star-sized one you'd be spaghettified into a stream of particles long before you ever reached the edge. And in your own (proper) time the fall takes only seconds, while a distant observer sees your light red-shift and freeze at the horizon forever — approaching it for infinite time, but never arriving.

Two black holes, 1.3 billion light-years away.

This is GW150914 — the first gravitational waves ever detected. Two black holes, about 36 and 29 times the Sun's mass, spiralled together and merged into one. In a fraction of a second the collision radiated more power than every star in the observable universe combined — as ripples in spacetime itself. LIGO caught that ripple on 14 Sep 2015, confirming a prediction Einstein made a century earlier, and earned the 2017 Nobel Prize in Physics.
